Abstract
This thesis explores the effectiveness of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks as interpretable function approximators in a Deep Reinforcement Learning context. We integrate Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks as
both actor and critic networks within the Proximal Policy Optimization
algorithm and benchmark their performances against Multi-Layer Perceptrons in two environments: CartPole and a Remote Electrical Tilt
scenario. By integrating structured pruning, spline regularisation, and
symbolic regression, Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks produce compact
policies characterised by sparse connectivity and interpretable symbolic expressions.
Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks agents match the performance of MultiLayer Perceptrons while requiring significantly fewer parameters. Moreover, Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks make the extraction of closed-form
symbolic policies possible, accurately approximating the learned behaviour and exposing the decision structure of the agent. In both environments, the symbolic outputs reflected the relevant input variables
and control strategies. While Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks introduce
additional training overhead, the resulting improvements in transparency
and compactness make them a promising alternative for Reinforcement Learning applications where interpretability is essential.

Keywords
Reinforcement Learning, Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks, Interpretability, Symbolic Policies, Proximal Policy Optimization, Radio Access Networks

1 Introduction
1.1 Motivation
The rapid evolution of mobile communications drives the development of increasingly intelligent and autonomous Radio Access Networks (RAN). As the industry progresses towards the sixth generation
(6G) of wireless technology, networks are expected to operate under
highly dynamic conditions, offering enhanced capacity, ultra-low latency, high reliability, and improved energy efficiency. Although the
precise specifications of 6G are still under development, it is widely acknowledged that Artificial Intelligence (AI) will be a cornerstone in its
architecture, enabling real-time adaptation, autonomous optimisation,
and cross-layer orchestration [1].
Within this context, RL has emerged as a promising paradigm for
enabling data-driven control in RAN. RL agents can learn directly from
interaction with the environment, optimising long-term objectives such
as quality of service, load balancing, or coverage, without relying on
explicit system models or hand-crafted heuristics. Recent studies have
demonstrated the potential of RL to handle complex decision-making
tasks such as beam forming [2], and antenna tilt optimisation [3].
However, despite the success of RL in simulated environments, its
real-world adoption remains limited. One of the significant barriers
is the lack of interpretability of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)
models, which are typically based on Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP).
These black-box architectures make it difficult for network operators
and engineers to understand the reasoning behind control decisions,
raising concerns about safety, reliability, and compliance.
This issue is becoming increasingly relevant as regulations such as

1

the European Union’s AI Act1 emphasize the need for transsparency
and human oversight in the deployment of AI-based systems, especially
in critical infrastructure like telecommunications. The Act promotes
the development of explainable and trustworthy AI, requiring models
to be understandable by human stakeholders and their decisions to be
justifiable.
The field of Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL) is gaining
traction to address this gap. XRL aims to provide insights into the behaviour of RL agents, either through post-hoc explanations or by designing inherently interpretable models [4]. While post-hoc methods
like saliency maps or surrogate models can offer limited insight, they
often fall short in reliability and completeness, especially in non-linear
and non-stationary dynamics typical of wireless environments.
A promising alternative is to embed interpretability directly into the
model architecture. KAN are a recent innovation that replaces fixed
non-linear activations in neural networks with learnable spline-based
functions. This design offers expressive power and the ability to extract
symbolic representations of the learned behaviour, enabling humanreadable policies.
In this thesis, we explore the integration of KAN within deep reinforcement learning for RAN control tasks. Specifically, we investigate
the use of KAN as actor and critic networks within the Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO) framework, evaluating their effectiveness in delivering high-performing, sparse, and symbolically interpretable policies.
By focusing on electrical RET, an important control problem in RAN,
we aim to assess whether KANs can help bridge the gap between learning performance and model transparency in realistic wireless network
environments.

1.2 Research Questions
To investigate this, we formulate the following research questions:
• RQ1: Can Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks be effectively integrated
into policy and value networks for Reinforcement Learning?
• RQ2: Do Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks-based Reinforcement Learning agents achieve competitive performance compared to conventional MLP-based agents in realistic network scenarios such as Remote Electrical Tilt?
• RQ3: To what extent do Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks enhance
the interpretability of Reinforcement Learning policies?
• RQ4: What computational trade-offs (e.g., training time, convergence behaviour) are introduced by Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks, and are they acceptable in practice?
These questions guide the theoretical, algorithmic, and experimental components of this work. Our goal is not only to validate KAN as a
functional alternative but also to explore their impact on the broader
agenda of trustworthy and transparent AI for mobile networks.

1.3 Ethics and Environmental Impact
Integrating interpretable models such as Kolmogorov-Arnold
Networks promotes ethical Artificial Intelligence by promoting
transparency, auditability, and regulatory compliance in critical
infrastructure. By enabling symbolic policies with fewer parameters,
these models reduce computational overhead, potentially lowering
the energy consumption of deployment. This contributes to more
sustainable and responsible Artificial Intelligence systems in future
wireless networks.

2 Background
2.1 Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks
Deep learning models were leading the surge of interest in AI and
Machine Learning (ML) in recent years, which demonstrated unmatched
success across a wide range of domains, from natural language processing and computer vision to scientific computing and control problems. At the core of most of these models lie MLP [5, 6], a class of
feed-forward neural networks acclaimed for their ability to approximate complex non-linear functions to an arbitrary degree of precision.
Despite their widespread adoption and recognised success, they are
often criticised for their lack of interpretability; indeed, they are frequently referred to as black-box models, limiting their practical application in fields where understanding the internal decision-making process is as essential as achieving high predictive accuracy.
To address this trade-off, a new neural network architecture, namely
the Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN), has been proposed by Liu
et al. [7]. While MLP rely on the Universal Approximation Theorem
(UAT) as their mathematical backbone, KAN draw direct inspiration
from a well-known result in real analysis, the Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem [8]. The theorem posits that any multivariate
continuous function can be represented as, i.e. it is equal to, a finite
composition of univariate continuous functions and addition operations. The theoretical implications of this result are powerful: it indicates that high-dimensional functions can, in principle, be decomposed into sums of simpler one-dimensional ones. KAN operationalise
this intuition by fixing univariate learnable functions along the edges
of the network, rather than using fixed non-linearities at the nodes, as
is the case with traditional MLP.

5

This new architecture has several consequences. First, it eliminates
the need for linear weight matrices, as each edge becomes a learnable
one-dimensional function, typically parametrised by basis spline class
functions. Second, it introduces inherent interpretability: the network
now relies on a finite set of univariate functions, which can be easily
analysed, visualised, and even symbolically approximated.
In this chapter, we lay out a systematic introduction to KAN, outlining their mathematical foundation, architectural design, and specific
distinctive features that make KAN particularly suited to an Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) framework. Many of the concepts presented in this chapter originate from the original work of Liu et al. [7],
which introduced the KAN architecture and developed its theoretical
and practical advantages over conventional MLP.
We organise the remainder of the chapter as follows. We begin by
discussing the Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem and its implications in the context of function approximation. This is then followed by an analysis of the Universal Approximation Theorem, to outline the traditional role of Multi-Layer Perceptrons within the field of
Neural Networks and Deep Learning. We then introduce the KAN architecture in detail, clarifying how activation functions are parametrised
and how they support the construction of rich yet transparent representations. We examine how design choices such as activation functions,
loss regularisation, and sparsification influence the interpretability of
KAN.

2.1.1 Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem
The Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem (KART) [8] is a foundational result in fundamental analysis and approximation theory. It
was initially proved by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1957 and later refined
by his student Vladimir Arnold in 1958, giving the theorem its name.
The theorem states that any continuous multivariate function can be
decomposed into a superposition of continuous unidimensional functions. This result is closely related to Hilbert’s 13th problem, for which

p = 1, . . . , n, such that:

This representation decomposes a general multivariate function into
a summation of transformed sums of univariate functions, effectively
reducing the problem of high-dimensional function approximation to
learning a finite number of one-dimensional functions.
Figure 1 provides a pictorial representation of the KART. Each ϕq,p
denotes a univariate activation function applied to the input variables
x1 and x2 , while each Φq represents another univariate function that
aggregates the inner summations. The black dots indicate summation
nodes, which compute the sum of all incoming inputs.
The implications of this theorem are both profound and subtle. From
one perspective, the theorem provides a strong theoretical guarantee:
it is possible to characterise any continuous multivariate function using only functions of a single variable and addition; this highlights a
promising direction for simplifying the learning of high-dimensional
functions. On the other hand, the KART is an existence result, meaning
that while it guarantees the existence of functions Φq and ϕq,p , it does
not provide a constructive method for explicitly determining them. Consequently, the theorem does not offer a direct means of obtaining these
functions, limiting its immediate applicability in machine learning contexts.
Thus, for many years, the KART was recognised primarily as a result
of theoretical interest, with little applicability in machine learning [9].
Many classical ML methods, including MLP, rely on smooth and dif-

However, recent advances, including the introduction of KAN and

This reconceptualisation offers a practical implementation of the KART,

Overall, while the original KART does not provides any explicit guidelines on how to construct the component functions in a ML friendly

2.1.2 Universal Approximation Theorem
The Universal Approximation Theorem (UAT). [11] is the foundational
result in the theory of neural networks. It provides a formal guarantee that MLP, one of the most used types of Artificial Neural Networks
(ANN), can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary precision. This theorem supports much of the theoretical confidence in using Deep Learning (DL), from pattern recognition and function regression to control and RL.

with a single hidden layer, containing a finite number of neurons and

they offer a transparent approach to function approximation, closely
aligning with how many physical and engineering systems are expected
to behave.

2.1.5 Interpretability in KANs
One of the most convincing rationales for using KAN, rather than MLP,
lies in their intrinsic interpretability property. While standards NN
achieve state-of-the-art performances at the cost of transparency, KAN
are designed with structural properties that make them more suitable
for analysis and simplification. The key insight is that non-linearities
are introduced into the network through univariate function transformations, making it possible to independently reason about, visualise,
and regularise these components. That said, the full interpretability
potential of a KAN becomes evident only when certain additional strate-

16

gies and regularisations are applied during training and post processing.

2.1.5.1 Sparsity and Pruning
Our initial observation is that smaller networks are usually more interpretable than larger ones. When the architecture has fewer nodes
per layer or fewer edge connections, the number of components to be
analysed and understood is significantly reduced. However, excessive
downsizing of the network can mine its expressiveness and ability to
approximate complex functions. To address this trade-off, KAN make
use of sparsity regularisation terms during training, which allows us to
train a larger network and then reduce the number of active components, so that we end up with the minimal size of the network that can
solve our task.
The first such regularisation term is an L1 penalty applied to the outputs of the activation functions. Since each function ϕi,j (x) contributes
to a successor node via summation, a function that remains close to
zero across the input distribution has a negligible impact on the model’s
output. By penalising the L1 -norm of these activations across training
samples, the model is encouraged to suppress functions that are not impactful, effectively pushing them toward being inactive, i.e. their representation becomes the zero function, φ(x) ≈ 0. This leads to natural
sparsification of the architecture: a fraction of the edge functions can
become inactive and subsequently can be pruned from the network.

2.1.5.2 Symbolification
Beyond pruning, the final and powerful interpretability step is symbolification. Because each active transformation in the network is a
univariate function defined over a spline basis, it becomes possible to
approximate it using a symbolic analytical expression. The goal of this
process is to replace a learned spline function ϕ with a mathematically
recognisable form, such as a linear, polynomial, trigonometric, exponential, or logarithmic function, whenever such a match exists.
However, since the inputs and outputs of spline functions can be
arbitrarily scaled or shifted during training, directly replacing ϕ with
a symbolic function f may result in a mismatch. To account for this,
the symbolification process searches for an affine transformation of the
symbolic function that best matches the learned activation. More precisely, we aim to find constants a, b, c, d ∈ R such that the following
approximation holds over the activation’s domain:
y ≈ c · f (ax + b) + d
where x and y are, respectively, the pre-activation and post-activation

20

values observed from the trained network during the training phase.
Once (x, y) pairs are obtained, the parameters a, b, c, d are fitted using a combination of grid search and linear regression. Grid search is
typically used to explore a discrete set of candidate scalings and shifts
(a, b), while for each such pair, the optimal (c, d) is computed via leastsquares regression to minimise the residual error between the transformed symbolic function and the learned spline output.
When a sufficiently accurate fit is found, i.e. the coefficient of determination R2 between c · f (ax + b) + d and y is greater than a predefined threshold, usually 0.9 or 0.95, the learned spline activation ϕ is
replaced with its symbolic approximation x 7→ c · f (ax + b) + d. This
replacement offers a way to recover a human-understandable explanation for part of the model’s internal computation.
When applied to the entire sparse KAN, this process yields a network composed of symbolic functions composed through addition and
function composition. The final result is a predictive model that is not
only performant but also transparent, one in which each transformation can be plotted, understood, and reasoned about analytically.

2.2 Reinforcement Learning
Reinforcement Learning [13] is an area of machine learning that
characterises how autonomous intelligent agents can learn to make sequential decisions by interacting with an environment. In contrast to
supervised learning, where models are trained on input-output pairs,
RL relies on a trial-and-error approach: agents explore actions, receive
feedback, and attempt to maximise a long-term goal by learning a behaviour. This paradigm is particularly suitable for environments where
decisions in the present can impact decisions in the future, and where
there is a trade-off between short-term and long-term advantage, such
as in robotics [14] and video game playing [15].
In the conventional RL framework, an agent observes the current
state of the environment, then selects an action and receives a reward,
modelled as a scalar value. After the action is performed, the agent observes the resulting transition, and this process is repeated. Over time,
the goal is to learn a policy, i.e. a mapping from states to actions, that
represents the agent’s behaviour such that the cumulative reward is
maximised. This interaction is illustrated in Figure 5. The process naturally captures delayed consequences, uncertainty, and strategic planning, making RL a natural framework for learning in dynamic and uncertain environments.

Recent developments in DRL have successfully scaled RL to high-

as function approximators, as illustrated in Figure 6. These methods

Classical DRL algorithms typically rely on approximating the value

state-of-the-art method known for its performance across various complex domains and environments.
The core idea behind PPO is to directly model the policy function by
performing constrained updates of the function itself. This avoids the
instabilities and scalability limitations of earlier policy methods, such
as REINFORCE [18] and Trust Region Policy Optimisation [19]. This
approach allows for a natural integration with KAN, facilitating clearer
interpretation of the learnt policies.

2.2.1 Markov Decision Processes
A common assumption in RL is that the environment dynamics can be
formalised as a MDP, a mathematical methodology for modelling environments where outcomes are influenced by both the agent’s actions
as well as the stochasticity of the dynamics itself.
Formally, a MDP is defined by the tuple
M = (S, A, P, R, γ),
where:
• S is a (possibly infinite) set of states.
• A is a set of actions available to the agent.
• P : S × A × S → [0, 1] is the state transition probability function.
For any s, s0 ∈ S and a ∈ A, the quantity
P(s0 | s, a) = P(st+1 = s0 | st = s, at = a)
specifies the probability of transitioning to state s0 after taking action a in state s.
• R : S ×A×S → R is the reward function, where R(s, a, s0 ) denotes
the expected immediate reward received after executing action a
in state s.

25

• γ ∈ [0, 1) is the discount factor, which determines the present
value of future rewards by geometrically diminishing their contribution over time.

At each discrete time step t, the agent observes the current state st ∈
S and selects an action at ∈ A according to a policy π, which can be
deterministic or stochastic. If the policy is stochastic, it is defined as a
conditional probability distribution:
π(a | s) = P(at = a | st = s).

interaction results in a trajectory (or episode) denoted by

2.2.3 Proximal Policy Optimization
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [17] is a state-of-the-art policygradient RL algorithm that combines the stability of trust-region methods with the scalability of policy-gradient approaches. It aims to improve the policy in a way that avoids large, destabilising updates, which
are a common issue in vanilla policy-gradient methods. PPO achieves
this by introducing a clipped surrogate objective that limits the deviation of the new policy from the old one during each update.
In this thesis, we implement PPO for discrete action spaces using a
modular actor-critic architecture. The actor is a neural policy network
parametrising a categorical distribution over actions, while the critic

2.3 Interpretability
With the rise in complexity of machine learning models, uncovering
the reasoning behind their outputs becomes more difficult. Moreover,
the RL framework introduces an additional layer of complexity, where
agents operate over time and learns while interacting with a stochastic
environment. Whereas supervised learning models learn static maps
between input and output, RL agents take sequential decisions and
optimise long-term rewards, relying on complex policies often opaque
even to domain experts. This lack of transparency in critical domains,
such as healthcare, autonomous systems, and telecommunication, presents
a significant obstacle to trust, debugging and adoption.
Despite the central role of interpretability in the ML community,
there is no uniform accepted definition of the term itself. The literature is fragmented, using various similar but distinct terms, such as interpretability, transparency, and explainability, often interchangeably.
This ambiguity originates from the absence of a formal or universally
accepted definition and a subjective usage: different authors make subtle distinctions without defining a common framework.
To contextualise our approach, we adopt the perspective introduced
by Puiutta and Veith [4] and start by examining the linguistic definitions of the words interpretation and explanation. While both convey
meaning, explanation focuses on making something easier to understand, often by offering reasons or causes. In contrast, interpretation
also involves choosing how to convey that information meaningfully
and clearly. This distinction is central in Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI), where the goal is not only to extract the right information
from a given model, but also to present it in a comprehensible form to
humans. Consequently, we adopt the following definition for this thesis, as stated by Puiutta and Veith. [4, p. 5]:
Interpretability is the ability not only to extract or generate
explanations for the decisions of a model, but also to present
this information in a way that is understandable by human

31

(non-expert) users, to enable them to anticipate and predict
the model’s behaviour.

2.3.1 General Taxonomy of Interpretability
Interpretability in ML is commonly categorised along two key dimensions: the timing of the explanation, intrinsic vs. post-hoc, and its target, local vs. global. This classification, established by Puiutta and Veith [4],
provides a theoretical foundation for understanding the diverse methods and scopes used in XAI.
The contrast between intrinsic and post-hoc interpretability describes
when the explanation is obtained. In the case of intrinsic interpretability, we refer to models that are intrinsically explainable by design, through
architectural design choices. Linear regression, decision trees and rulebased models are all examples of intrinsic models. The inference process in these models inherently generates interpretable explanations.
Nevertheless, these models typically achieve interpretability through
architectural simplicity, which limits their predictive performance and,
consequently, restricts their applicability to complex problems. On the
other hand, post-hoc interpretability refers to methods that generate
explanations after our model is trained, and in fact, these methods are
model agnostic. Here we find methods like saliency maps, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [21] and LIME (Local Interpretable Modelagnostic Explanations) [22]. These methods can, in principle, be applied to black-box models, but their truthfulness in generating explanations is not always guaranteed.
Along the second axis, this taxonomy differentiates between local
and global explanations. As their names suggest, their goal is to provide either instance-specific explanations (local) or general overall ones
(global). Local explanations outline why the underlying model made a
specific prediction for a particular instance. Global explanations, instead, attempt to generalise the overall behaviour of the model, trying
to understand the logic or pattern that applies to all the input space.
Local explanations improve trust in specific predictions. Global inter-

32

pretability, in turn, supports system-wide comprehension and facilitates auditing.
This taxonomy, though foundational, is not capturing the additional
complexities that arise in RL tasks, where other dynamics and tradeoffs are encountered.

2.3.2 A Taxonomy for Explainable Reinforcement Learning
RL incorporates a unique layer of complexity: here we are computing
a map between input states and output actions, and in these mappings
are contained not only the environment stochastic dynamics, but also
all the trade-offs typical of RL, namely exploration-exploitation and
short-term vs long-term rewards. Consequently, the taxonomy introduced in section 2.3.1 cannot account for the temporal stochastic dynamic typical of RL policies.
To address this gap, Milani et al. [23] propose a taxonomy specifically tailored to XRL, identifying three collectively exhaustive interpretability classes:
• Feature importance explanations, emphasise the most influential
features that shaped a particular decision of our agent. These explanations are local, explaining why an agent took action at in state
st by analysing which component of st led to that decision.
• Learning process and MDP explanations, focus more on the behaviour of the environment and which specific dynamics and/or
instances shaped the overall agent behaviour. This involves emphasising influential past experiences involving rewards, decisions
and transitions. These explanations can be either local or global,
depending on whether they are focusing on the overall trend of the
agent or on specific instances.
• Policy-level explanations offer insight into the long-term behaviour
of the agent. Because these explanations are global ones, they
should exclude details for the sake of interpretability.

33

Figure 7 illustrates the explained triad of explainability dimensions
in XRL, feature importance, learning process and MDP, and policylevel explanations described above. This factorisation reveals that understanding a RL requires not only to reason about individual actions,
but also to understand the overall goal of the agent and the dynamic
itself. Hence, explainability in RL must operate at multiple semantic
levels.

2.3.3 Interpretability in Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks
for Reinforcement Learning
Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks offer an architecture particularly wellsuited for XRL. The design of KAN, based on compositional and hierarchical structure, supports interpretability at both structural and functional levels. This section analyses which elements of the interpretability taxonomies KAN support, highlighting both their contributions and
limitations.
Once the network is pruned and sparse, each edge function can be

34

symbolified, i.e. approximated by symbolic regression. This replaces
splines with a known, closed-form expression. These expressions provide not only a post-hoc interpretation of the network’s decisions, but
also a global picture of the policy’s long-term behaviour and goal, highlighting the most influential input states in the overall decisions and
specific subregions of interest. This makes it possible to reason analytically on the agent’s behaviour, and can be used to analyse particular
decisions and behaviour in borderline situations, for instance.
Referring to the triad in the XRL taxonomy, KAN support feature
importance explanations by enabling straightforward identification of
the contribution of specific features to a given decision. Moreover, the
impact of each input on the policy’s output can be assessed directly.
KAN also excel in policy-level explanations, as they allow the extraction of symbolic representations of the value function or policy. These
symbolic formulas provide both a global summary of the agent’s behavior, highlighting which features most strongly influence overall decision and a means of analysing behavior in specific sub-regions of interest, particularly in borderline situations, by inspecting which features
are most influential and which ones may drive the agent toward a different decision when slightly perturbed.
Finally, KAN can be used to understand what drives the learning
process, in particular, we can see how activation functions evolve
during training, starting from a dense architecture towards and see
which features gets flatten out or removed, hence highlighting how
the agent’s strategy matures. This visibility into the learning dynamics
is rarely accessible in conventional architectures and offers a unique
opportunity to interpret how the model discards irrelevant
information.

3 Methodology
3.1 Problem Setting and Objectives
This chapter formally defines the research problem and outlines the
objectives addressed in this work. Building upon the prior theoretical
and architectural foundation of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks, the focus is now on integrating this architecture into a Reinforcement Learning framework in a way that explicitly promotes interpretability.
Despite the fact that Kich et al. [24] already applied KAN with Proximal Policy Optimization, their focus was on performance metrics, and
did not attempt to recover, interpret or analyse the resulting policy.
Conversely, our central aim is to leverage the peculiar spline-based architecture of KAN to extract and analyse meaningful explanations of an
agent’s model behaviour. This approach extends beyond raw performances, with the goal of obtaining interpretable, and potentially symbolic, policies that can improve trustworthiness and enhance humanin-the-loop decision making in RL.
The main goal of our work is to integrate KAN with policy-gradient
methods, specifically Proximal Policy Optimization, and evaluate whether
this combination can yield interpretable policies without compromising learning performance.
This work introduces a KAN-based PPO training pipeline that incorporates:
• A sparsity regularisation scheme based on L1 and entropy penalties over learned spline activations, as discussed in Subsection 2.1.5.1;
• Periodic pruning to eliminate inactive spline functions and reduce
architectural complexity;

37

• Post-pruning symbolification, in which active spline transformations are approximated with closed-form expressions via symbolic
regression, as discussed in Subsection 2.1.5.2.
Together, these steps are designed to produce compact and humaninterpretable policies, where each transformation can be visualised in
isolation and the overall policy can be understood as a whole. The resulting agent serves as a link between DRL and interpretability.
To assess the effectiveness of our approach, experiments are first
conducted in the CartPole environment, a well-known RL and control
benchmark environment. This setting allows for qualitative examination of policy behaviours, serving as a reliable step for debugging.
The intended final application is the control and optimisation of
RAN, where interpretable RL agents could support autonomous
decision-making in dynamic and high-stakes telecommunication
systems.

3.2 Environments
To assess the premises and goals of KAN in the context of XRL, we
consider two different environments which differ in complexity and
task goal. The first scenario is a standard and well-known environment,
namely CartPole, which will help us conduct a preliminary analysis and
validate our training pipeline. The second is a real-world-inspired simulation environment, designed to mimic a Radio Access Networks scenario, representing the intended deployment domain for interpretable
decision-making.

3.2.1 CartPole
Cartpole is a classic benchmark, originally introduced by Barto, Sutton, and Anderson in their seminal work “Neuronlike Adaptive Elements That Can Solve Difficult Learning Control Problems” [25]. The
principal use of this environment is to evaluate and compare learning
algorithms in solving a classic control problem.
The task involves balancing a pole mounted on a cart that moves
along the horizontal axis. At each time step, the agent must choose
between pushing the cart to the left or right, aiming to prevent the pole
from falling. The environment is defined by a four dimensional state
space:
• Cart position x, representing the horizontal displacement of the
cart from the centre of the track,
• Cart velocity ẋ, indicating how fast the cart is moving,
• Pole angle θ, measuring the deviation of the pole from the vertical
axis,
• Pole angular velocity θ̇, representing the rate of change of the pole
angle.

cart position stays within |xmax | = 2.4 units. As anticipated before, the
action space is discrete, consisting of two actions: pushing left or pushing right.

the standard interface gymnasium.make("CartPole-v1").

3.3 Agents architecture
All agents in this thesis are based on the actor-critic framework, a
class of RL methods in which two networks are trained simultaneously:
an actor, which learns the policy π(a | s), and a critic, which estimates
the value function V (s). We adopt the Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm as the training backbone for both our KAN-based and baseline
agents as discussed in Subsection 2.2.3.
To support the use of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks within the Proximal Policy Optimization framework, the practical implementation relies on the open source PyKan library, which implements the splinebased activation functions and handles their gradient propagation, as
well as the symbolification step1 . On top of this, we implemented the
KANActor and KANCritic modules, adapting the base KAN class to the
PPO requirements. The PPO agent itself and its training loop were also
implemented from scratch.

3.3.1 KAN-Based Agent
In the proposed architecture, both the actor and critic networks are
implemented using KAN, which replace traditional MLP.
Beyond the standard PPO training procedure, KAN integrate regularisation terms designed to control the complexity and behaviour of
the learned representations, as discussed in subsection 2.1.5.1. These
regularisations serve both to improve generalisation and to preserve
interpretability, a key advantage of the KAN framework. Specifically:
• L1 regularisation is applied to the edge functions to promote simplification of the resulting functional graph.
• Entropy regularisation is introduced to promote less complex function shapes.
• Coefficient smoothness penalty is used to constrain the spline co1 Which relies itself on SymPy.

47

efficients of each edge function, reducing oscillatory behaviour and
abrupt changes.
Together, these regularisation penalties form a crucial component
of the training pipeline, guiding the network toward parsimonious and
semantically meaningful solutions. Their integration within the PPO
framework enables a seamless balance between expressive function approximation and structural simplicity.

3.3.2 MLP Baseline
To provide a fair baseline for comparison, we implement a PPO agent
using standard MLP for both actor and critic networks. The MLP are
designed to match the KAN architecture in terms of expressiveness,
typically using two hidden layers with ReLU activations and the same
number of hidden units per layer.
The MLP baseline does not include any symbolic or sparsity constraint, and is treated as a black-box model throughout the experiments.
Its role is to serve as a performance benchmark and as a reference for
evaluating the interpretability of the KAN-based agent.

5 Discussion
In this chapter, we interpret and contextualise the results presented
in Chapter 4. We examine the observed learning behaviours, model
structures, and symbolic outcomes across the evaluated environments.
The discussion is organised per environment, starting with the classical
CartPole control task, followed by the RAN scenario.

5.1 Discussion on the CartPole Environment
Training Efficiency and Learning Dynamics
The MLP agent reaches high performance quickly, while the KAN agent
exhibits slower convergence. This delay is expected, as KAN models are
trained with multiple regularisation terms that inhibit early aggressive
updates. Furthermore, spline evaluations are computationally more
intensive than matrix multiplications, and the PyKan library lacks the
low-level optimisation found in PyTorch’s MLP backend.
Despite slower training and longer wall-clock time (Figure 14), the
KAN agent ultimately reaches competitive performance. This highlights a key trade-off: MLPs offer training speed and simplicity, whereas
KANs introduce interpretability and built-in feature selection, at a modest computational cost.

Sparsity and Symbolic Structure
The pruning phase (Figure 18) reveals that the agent learns to ignore
the cart position x and velocity ẋ, relying only on θ and θ̇. This aligns
with physical intuition: balancing the pole depends more directly on

72

its angular state than on the cart’s position.
The symbolic expressions in Equation 4.1 offer a transparent, interpretable description of the learned policy. The smooth tanh-based
terms match the continuous control nature of the task. The decision
boundary (Figure 15) is symmetric and non-linear, illustrating that the
agent respects the system’s structure while introducing nonlinear corrections to optimise behavior.
This symbolic output provides a human-readable alternative to blackbox neural policies, supporting applications where transparency, verifiability, or safety constraints are crucial.

5.2 Discussion on the RAN Environment
Training Efficiency and Learning Dynamics
The training dynamics observed in the RAN environment show similar
behaviour between KAN and MLP agents in terms of rewards and final
performances, Figure 20 and Figure 23 respectively. The main differences can be seen in the number of parameters used, Table 6, where
KAN had ≈ 40 times fewer parameters, but needed ≈ 25% more training time, Table 21.
The complexity of the spine-based activation functions primarily introduces this training overhead. This overhead is reflected in the training time distribution, Figure 21, where KAN showed a consistent increase in computational time compared to MLP.
Despite this initial overhead, KAN agents showed robust and stable learning dynamics, reflected in a consistent improvement and then
plateau, of training rewards, Figure 20. By contrast, MLP showed a
more volatile training behaviour.
An essential step in the training loop was the symbolification process, Figure 22, where a symbolic expression replaced the spline-based
functions. This phase resulted in a minimal performance degradation,
demonstrating that the symbolic approximation accurately captured

73

the decision-making behaviour learned by the network. Moreover, the
shift to symbolic representation substantially enhanced interpretability, without sacrificing performance.

Sparsity and Symbolic Structure
An unexpected outcome emerged during the hyperparameter tuning
phase: the best-performing KAN model was the smallest, featuring no
hidden layers, and involving just 112 trainable parameters. As a result,
the potential for sparsification was limited due to the already minimal
architecture. Despite this, some pruning still occurred, as illustrated in
Figure 27.
The resulting symbolic structure was simplified, reflecting a direct
and precise mapping between input variables and actions. This minimalistic symbolic structure highlights the key idea of KAN, showcasing their ability to learn minimal and meaningful relationships that not
only domain experts can interpret and understand. The simplicity and
clarity of the final symbolic expressions reinforce their value in deployment scenarios where interpretability, trust, and ease of analysis are
essential.

Interpretation of the Policy in a Physical Context
The final policy, as shown in Equation 4.1 and plotted in Figure 25,
provides an explicit and interpretable mapping between the two network performance metrics, Signal to Interference plus Noise Ratio and
Reference Signal Received Power, and the corresponding tilt actions.
Understanding these metrics in their physical terms is essential to appreciate the rationale behind the policy behaviour.
SINR refers to the quality of the received signal. A high SINR means
a strong signal, typically requiring little to no adjustment. Conversely,
low SINR indicates a noisy environment, where interference impacts
signal quality, calling for adjustments to antenna tilt angle.
RSRP refers instead to the strength of the signal measured at the

74

UEs. Higher RSRP values suggest a stable and strong signal reception,
typically requiring minimal or no adjustments. Lower RSRP values indicate a weaker signal, resulting in a poor coverage or connectivity issue, demanding tilt adjustments.
The policy derived from the chosen KAN model, Equation 4.1, aligns
with these physical intuitions. As shown in Figure 25, region with lower
SINR and/or lower RSRP initiate explicit tilt adjustments of ±1◦ . By
contrast, areas characterised by higher SINR and RSRP require no adjustments, reflecting the optimal conditions of the environment.
Beyond the policy behaviour, this workflow is a compelling proof
of concept. It demonstrates model performance and interpretability,
strengthening confidence in its applicability to real-world Radio Access
Networks.

6 Conclusion
This chapter addresses each research question posed in the Introduction, summarising the contributions and key findings of the thesis.
RQ1: Can Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks be effectively integrated into policy and value networks for Reinforcement
Learning?
Yes. This thesis demonstrated that Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks
can be effectively integrated into the Proximal Policy Optimization framework as both actor and critic networks.
KAN were successfully trained in the CartPole and RAN environments. In both cases, KAN based agents learned policies that matched
or exceeded the performance of the corresponding MLP based baselines. Regularisation enabled the KAN to converge to sparse representations without compromising convergence stability or learning dynamics.
RQ2: Do Kolmogorov-Arnold Network-based Reinforcement Learning agents achieve competitive performance compared to conventional MLP-based agents in realistic network
scenarios such as Remote Electrical Tilt?
The experimental results demonstrate that KAN agents can achieve
competitive performance compared to conventional MLP agents. In
both the CartPole and RAN environments, the best KAN models reached
final performance levels comparable to those of the strongest MLP baselines, despite using ≈ ×40 less parameters.
For instance, in CartPole, a KAN actor with only 126 trainable pa-

77

rameters achieved the same cumulative reward as an MLP actor with
8385 parameters. In the RAN environment, similar trends were observed: the selected KAN configuration matched the MLP baseline in
terms of final reward while offering a substantially smaller and sparser
model.
These results confirm that KAN can serve as lightweight and performant alternatives to MLP in both benchmark and realistic RL tasks.
RQ3: To what extent do Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks enhance the interpretability of Reinforcement Learning policies?
One of KAN’ main advantages is their ability to yield interpretable
representations. This thesis assessed interpretability primarily through
symbolic policy extraction and decision boundary visualisation.
After regularised training and pruning, KAN policies could be expressed as compact symbolic formulas involving only a subset of the
original input features. These formulas provided transparent, humanreadable descriptions of the decision logic. In CartPole and RAN, the
symbolic expressions reflected meaningful control behaviour and offered clear insight into which variables the agent relied on.
Furthermore, decision boundary visualisations confirmed that the
symbolic policies captured the essential structure of the learned action space. Compared to conventional MLP, which act as black boxes,
KAN offer a significant improvement in policy interpretability, especially when sparsity and symbolic extraction are properly enforced during training.
In addition, KAN enable snapshot inspection of the model throughout training, showing the evolution of the learned representations. This
introspection reveals how the network gradually refines its focus, discarding irrelevant inputs while retaining salient features. Such transparency into the learning dynamics is typically inaccessible in standard

78

architectures and provides a unique opportunity to understand and debug the model’s internal mechanisms.
RQ4: What computational trade-offs are introduced by KolmogorovArnold Networks, and are they acceptable in practice?
KANs introduce moderate computational overhead compared to conventional MLPs, primarily due to the spline-based edge functions and
regularisation terms used during training. In both the CartPole and
RAN environments, KANs agents required longer training times per
episode and per update cycle.
Despite these costs, the training remained stable and converged reliably across different seeds and configurations. The benefits of increased interpretability and reduced model size may offset the added
complexity in some particular use cases. In particular, KANs achieved
similar or better performance than MLPs while using an order of magnitude fewer parameters.
Overall, the computational trade-offs introduced by KANs may be
acceptable in domains where model transparency and compactness
are critical design requirements.
